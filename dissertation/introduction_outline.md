# Introduction chapter: section structure

This is the planned structure for Chapter 1 (Introduction). It is the skeleton
only, agreed before drafting. The prose is written after Meeting 2, once the
direction is confirmed, and in my own voice. The backbone follows the elements I
set out (problem statement, motivation, verification gap, research question,
contribution, dissertation outline). I have added a short opening context
section and a scope section, the latter because the supervisor asked for the
two-class decision to be reflected in the Introduction.

## 1.1 Background and context
Sets the scene: generative AI is now widely used in student writing, and that
puts academic integrity under new pressure. One short paragraph to orient the
reader before the problem is stated.

## 1.2 Problem statement
Current AI-text detectors return a single percentage with no reasoning, which a
lecturer cannot defend if challenged, and which risks unfair accusations. The
deeper issue is that a flag does not tell you whether the student understands
their own submission.

## 1.3 Motivation
Why this matters in practice: lecturers on large modules (150 to 300 students)
have no time to verify each flagged submission by hand, and need something
transparent, fair, and defensible.

## 1.4 The verification gap
The specific gap this project fills: nothing today turns a detection flag into
specific, source-grounded verification questions. The system bridges detection
and a check of genuine understanding.

## 1.5 Research question and objectives
States the primary research question, then breaks it into concrete objectives
(transparent detection, explanation of flags, argument mining, question
generation with two backends, Bloom's quality control, and the evaluation).

## 1.6 Scope and boundaries
What is in scope (two-class Human vs AI, the six pipeline components, the named
datasets) and what is deliberately out (no intermediate classes, no human
participants). Records the move from multi-class to two-class and why.

## 1.7 Contributions
The claimed contributions: an explainable detection-to-verification pipeline,
argument-aware question generation with provenance, a comparison of a local
open-source model against a commercial one, an objective discrimination-based
evaluation, and a fairness analysis across native and non-native writers.

## 1.8 Dissertation outline
One line per following chapter (Literature Review, Methodology, Implementation,
Evaluation, Discussion, Conclusion) so the reader knows the shape of the document.
